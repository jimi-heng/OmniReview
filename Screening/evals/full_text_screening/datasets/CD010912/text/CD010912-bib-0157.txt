The evidence base linking physical inactivity with 2 mental illness, and physical activity with mental health and well-being, is also accumulating 3 (e.g., Penedo & Dahn, 2005) . For example, synthesizing the literature on the acute effects of 4 exercise on energy in healthy populations, Loy, O'Connor and Dishman (2013) found a 5 consistently positive effect of (mainly low-to-moderate intensity) exercise on energy levels, 6 although no consistent effect of acute bouts of exercise on fatigue. Another meta-analysis 7 conducted by Puetz, O'Connor and Dishman (2006) reported a moderate effect of physical 8 activity on increases in energy and reductions in fatigue, although 77% of the effects were 9 based on studies conducted with patient samples. Similarly, Reed and Ones (2006) and Reed 10 and Buck (2009) reported increases in high activation positive affect (e.g., energy) from even 11 low to moderate intensity exercise training over 10-12 weeks (Cohen's d = .47), with those 12 with low energy scores at baseline showing larger effects (Cohen's d = .63). Physical activity 13 may change affective experiences in different spheres of life, such as work. The hypotheses were largely supported. 10 Specifically, beyond any influence of positive and negative affectivity, equivalent traits, work 11 motivation and perceived workload, participants in the IT group reported greater levels of 12 enthusiasm and relaxation at work in the afternoon compared to participants in the DT 13 condition (DT-C phase) observed in the afternoon of the same day. Walking therefore seems 14 to have both energising and relaxing properties in the workplace, which supports the main 15 hypothesis of the study. The within-person analysis comparing afternoon responses on 16 walking versus non-walking days confirmed the results of the between-group analysis as 17 afternoons following walks were associated with greater levels of enthusiasm and relaxation. 18 Given recent findings from the meta-analysis by Ford et al. ( 2011 ) that affective experiences 19 at work are linked to workplace performance, these results may have broader implications for 20 the success of companies. 21 However, the additional within-person analysis revealed a different pattern of results. 22 Specifically, nervousness decreased from the mornings to the afternoons on walking days in 23 both groups. Thus, a 30 minute group walk appears to have stress reducing properties in 24 physically inactive employees. A similar pattern was found when we compared the afternoons 25 The present study builds on the research by showing nervousness can be reduced within the 7 same day as a result of a lunchtime walk. 8 The findings related to fatigue were largely supportive of the hypotheses. Specifically, 9 there were no group differences in fatigue in the afternoons when the IT group had walked 10 during lunchtime. The within-person analyses also showed no differences in fatigue between 11 afternoons following walking activity compared to afternoons on non-walking days. These 12 results are in accordance with the recent meta-analysis conducted by Loy et al (2013) showing 13 inconsistent effects of acute bouts of low-to-moderate intensity exercise on fatigue. Although 14 a meta-analysis by Puetz et al. (2006) documented moderate sized decreases in fatigue 15 following chronic participation, most of the participants included in the analysis were patient 16 groups. It is possible that the effect of physical activity on fatigue differ between clinical and 17 non-clinical groups, with stronger effects for clinical populations. 18 However, when levels of fatigue were compared in the mornings versus afternoons on 19 walking days (H2), IT group participants experienced increases, while there were no changes 20 in the DT group. These differences between the groups were unexpected, but the finding may 21 be explained by seasonal variation. A recent EMA study by Kööts et al. (2011) has revealed 22 that momentary ratings of high levels of fatigue were more likely in cold and dark conditions. 23 This explanation may be relevant here as the IT group began their intervention in the cold 24 winter (February) months when the average temperature was 2.4 °C and the DT group started 25 their intervention phase in lighter spring conditions (May) when the average temperature was 1 10.7 °C.
