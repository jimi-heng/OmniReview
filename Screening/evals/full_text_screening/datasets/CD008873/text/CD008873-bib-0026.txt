INTRODUCTION Breastfed infants of vitamin D-sufficient mothers are protected from vitamin D-deficiency rickets during the first few months of life because vitamin D metabolites, especially 25hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], 11 cross the placenta, resulting in neonatal 25(OH)D concentrations that are approximately twothirds that of maternal 25(OH)D concentrations during pregnancy (1, 2) . Because the half-life of serum 25(OH)D is approximately 3 wk, neonates who do not receive an exogenous supply of vitamin D during the first weeks of life will experience decreases in their serum 25(OH)D concentrations from w2 mo of age (3) . Potential sources of vitamin D include UVB exposure, vitamin D supplements, infant milk formula, and breast milk (3) (4) (5) . Human milk from a vitamin D-sufficient mother provides ,100 IU total vitamin D activity (VDA) [i.e., concentration of vitamin D 2 , vitamin D 3 , 25(OH)D 2 , and 25(OH)D 3 ]/d (6) (7) (8) (9) . The recommendation to exclusively breastfeed infants to 6 mo of age has led several countries to introduce vitamin D supplementation of breastfed infants (10) . This supplementation allows exclusively breastfed infants to achieve vitamin D intakes $400 IU/d (10) . The possibility of supplementing the lactating mother has been considered as an alternative to supplementing the infant. Highdose vitamin D supplementation of the mother during lactation results in an increase in the total VDA of breast milk (11, 12) with 4000 IU/d being more effective than 2000 IU/d for increasing both maternal and infant serum 25(OH) concentrations and breast-milk VDA (11) . Another potential method for increasing the vitamin D concentration of breast milk at birth is by supplementing the mother during pregnancy and, thereby, providing adequate vitamin D to the breastfeeding neonate. Our aim was to examine the effect of vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy on breast-milk VDA in the first 2 mo of lactation. To our knowledge, there are no published data on this topic.METHODSStudy design, setting, and sample This study was performed within a randomized, doubleblinded, placebo-controlled trial of vitamin D 3 supplementation in pregnancy and infancy (13) in Auckland, New Zealand. Ethical approval was obtained from the Regional Ministry of Health Ethics Committee (Northern X committee; application NTX09/11/101). Written informed consent was obtained from all participating women. This trial was registered at the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry at ACTRN12610000483055. Two-hundred sixty pregnant women and their infants were randomly assigned to one of 3 groups as follows: a placebo group, a group who received one dosage of daily oral vitamin D 3 (1000 IU), or a group who received 2 dosages of daily oral vitamin D 3 (2000 IU) (Figure 1 ). More than 90% of the women reported adherence with the daily study medication protocol, and adherence did not differ between study groups (13) . We invited women who were enrolled in this trial and whose intention was to exclusively breastfeed to provide a breast-milk sample 2 wk and 2 mo postpartum (Figure 1 ).Data collection Face-to-face interviews were completed with women at enrollment at 27 wk of gestation, at 36 wk of gestation, and when their infants were 2 mo old. Data were collected on maternal age, ethnicity, education, weight and height, gestation, and adherence to the study medication. Maternal vitamin D-supplement use and the time spent outdoors were recorded. In New Zealand adults, reported sunlight exposure is correlated with the serum 25(OH) D concentration (14) . Maternal dietary vitamin D intake was determined from a food-frequency questionnaire (FFQ). The FFQ contained 12 food items that captured the major dietary sources of vitamin D in the New Zealand food supply, which were derived from the New Zealand Food Composition Database (15). The frequency of consumption over the previous 4 wk of each food item was recorded (with the use of standard foodserving sizes). Frequency responses were recorded as never, less than monthly, 1-3 times/mo, weekly, 2-4 times/wk, 5-6 times/ wk, daily, and $2 times/d. The frequency of consumption of each food item in the FFQ was converted to intake of vitamin D expressed as mg/d by multiplying the vitamin D concentration of the standard serving size of each food as specified in the FFQ by the following values for each frequency option: never or ,1 time/mo = 0; 1-3 times/mo = 0.1; 1 time/wk = 0.14; 2-4 times/ wk = 0.4; 5-6 times/ week = 0.8; 1 time/d = 1.0; and $2 times/ d = 2. Maternal serum 25(OH)D was measured at 27 and 36 wk of gestation with the use of isotope-dilution liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) in a vitamin D External Quality Assurance Scheme-certified laboratory (16) .Breast-milk sample collection Participants used a standard protocol for the collection of breast-milk samples for the subsequent analysis of vitamin D concentrations (17) . Samples were collected either manually or with the use of a human-milk pump. Breast milk was collected directly into a small sampling bottle that was provided to the mothers. If a pump was used, milk was collected into a clean container that came with the pump and was poured into the sampling bottle. Breasts and hands were cleaned, but soap and lotions were avoided because they may have contained chemicals that could interfere with the measurement of breast-milk VDA. The sample was collected at the regular feeding time in the morning, 2 h after the previous breastfeed (this method was used to allow for some consistency between samples). A full breast was expressed to ensure that the hindmilk was collected. The samples were stored in the participant's freezer until collection. Samples were transferred to a 2808C freezer before being shipped on dry ice to the laboratory for analyses.Breast-milk analyses and measurement of VDA Breast-milk VDA was assessed by summing the concentration of vitamin D 2 , vitamin D 3 , 25(OH)D 2 , and 25(OH)D 3 in breast milk and using the following conversion: 1 IU vitamin D = 25 ng. In addition, 1 IU 25(OH)D was equal to 5 ng and 1.030 g/mL for the specific gravity of milk (18) . Breast-milk concentrations of vitamin D 2 , vitamin D 3 , 25(OH)D 2 , and 25(OH)D 3 were determined with the use of LC-MS/MS techniques as described previously (Heartland Assays Inc.) (19) . Methanolic potassium hydroxide was added to each milk sample (5-10 mL) followed by spiking with [6, 19, 19-d 3 ]-vitamin D 3 and [6, 19, 19-d 3 ]-25(OH)D 3 . Samples were mixed on a vortex and saponified. After 0.5-2 h at 608C, the lipids were extracted with methylene chloride. The lipid extracts were dried under a vacuum and applied to a 0.5-g silica solid-phase extraction column. Vitamin D and 25(OH)D were eluted and collected separately from the solid-phase extraction column. Solvent was removed under a vacuum, and samples were purified with the use of 2 chemically different HPLC systems. The final quantitation of the vitamin D and 25(OH)D was achieved by LC-MS/MS with the use of an Agilent 1200 Series Rapid Resolution LC system (Agilent Technologies) coupled with an Agilent 6460 Triple Quadrupole LC/MS with multimode ionization (Agilent Technologies). Calibration curves were constructed for vitamin D and 25(OH)D. Excellent linearity was achieved for vitamin D and 25(OH)D with R 2 values that were very close to 1.00. The day-to-day (interassay and intra-assay) CVs for quantification were #12%. The limit of detection for each assay was 10 pg/mL.Statistical analyses With the use of the data from Hollis and Wagner (11) , we estimated that the SD of breast-milk VDA would be w12 IU/L. With 25 subjects in each treatment group, a difference of 12 IU/L between 2 supplementation amounts could be detected with 80% power at the 5% level of significance. Because distributions of VDA values were skewed, we used a logarithmic transformation to stabilize variances and lessen the effect of extreme values. Estimates were back transformed for reporting. All statistical analyses were performed with the use of SAS version 9.3 software (SAS Institute). To compare the VDA in breast milk collected at birth and 2 mo between the 3 treatment groups, we used a linear mixed model with the participant as the random effect and used an autoregressive correlation structure for the 2 time periods. Two orthogonal contrasts of the supplementation amounts allowed for the assessment of the placebo treatment compared with the active treatment and 1000 IU compared with 2000 IU. The model also included the following 3 potential confounding factors: ethnicity (in 4 categories), the time of year that the samples were taken, and the self-reported maternal sunlight exposure. The association between maternal serum 25(OH)D concentrations at 36 wk of gestation and breast-milk VDA was described with the use of Spearman correlation coefficients.RESULTS Breast-milk samples were collected from a total of 75 women (Figure 1 ), 44 of whom provided breast-milk samples at both 2 wk and 2 mo postpartum. The 31 women who provided one sample only were from the following 2 groups: 27 women who provided a sample at 2 wk (but not 2 mo) because they did not continue breastfeeding until 2 mo, and 4 women who provided a sample at 2 mo (but not 2 wk) because they had some difficulties establishing breast feeding (Figure 1 ). Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the 75 mothers who provided a breast-milk sample compared with the 118 mothers from whom a breast-milk sample was not obtained. In mothers who provided a breast-milk sample compared with in mothers who did not provide a breast-milk sample, the median maternal serum 25(OH)D concentration at 36 wk of gestation (97 compared with 77 nmol/L, respectively; P , 0.001) and median cord blood 25(OH)D concentration (64 compared with 46 nmol/L, respectively; P , 0.001) were higher . Table 2 shows the characteristics of the 75 study participants from whom breast-milk samples were obtained. The median maternal age at enrollment was 30 y, and gestation was 27 wk. Median serum 25(OH)D concentrations at 36 wk of gestation were higher in the supplemented groups than in the placebo group (110 compared with 71 nmol/L, respectively; P = 0.003) but did not differ between higher-and lower-dose-vitamin D groups (122 compared with 106 nmol/L, respectively; P = 0.24). Median serum 25(OH)D concentrations in cord blood samples differed between supplemented and placebo groups (66 compared with 44 nmol/L, respectively; P = 0.002) and between higher-and lower-dose-vitamin D groups (78 compared with 64 nmol/L, respectively; P = 0.03). There was no significant interaction between the samplecollection time and the study group on VDA (P = 0.61). The 2 orthogonal contrasts between the treatment groups showed that the VDA in the placebo group was not different from that in the vitamin D-supplemented groups (16% difference; 95% CI: 210%, 50% difference; P = 0.24), but the VDA in the higher-dose-vitamin D group was greater than that in the lower-dose-vitamin D group (39% difference; 95% CI: 4%, 87% difference; P = 0.04) To assess the effect of extreme values on the results, the analyses were repeated with the use of a series of VDA cutoffs to exclude extreme values. The only substantial effect that the removal of extreme values had was to expose a possible difference in the VDA between the placebo group and the groups who received vitamin D supplementation. When the 3 breast-milk samples with the highest VDA were removed from the analysis, the percentage difference between the vitamin D groups and the placebo group was 45% (95% CI: 14%, 83%; P = 0.002). With these 3 samples removed from the analysis, the percentage difference between the 1000-IU vitamin D and 2000-IU vitamin D groups became 30% (95% CI: 1%, 71%; P = 0.07). Of the 71 subjects who provided a breast-milk sample 2 wk postpartum, the 36-wk maternal serum 25(OH)D concentration was correlated with breast-milk VDA (r = 0.40, P , 0.001) (Figure 3 ). Of the 48 subjects who provided a breast-milk sample 2 mo postpartum, the correlation between the 36-wk maternal serum 25(OH)D concentration and breast-milk VDA at 2 mo postpartum was NS (r = 0.28, P = 0.06).DISCUSSION Analyses of breast-milk samples from women who were participating in a randomized trial suggested that the higher amount of vitamin D 3 supplementation (2000 IU/d) compared with the lower amount of vitamin D 3 supplementation (1000 IU/d) resulted in a higher amount of breast-milk VDA, but breast-milk VDA in the supplemented groups was not much greater than what was present in the women who had been randomly assigned to the placebo group. However, when the breast-milk samples with the 3 largest VDA values (.190 IU/L) were excluded from the analysis (2 samples from the placebo group and one sample from the 2000-IU/d group), the difference in VDA between the 2 vitamin D-supplemented groups was slightly reduced, whereas the placebo group had VDA values that were substantially below those that were present in the supplemented groups. Until more is known about the range of VDA values that are valid, we cannot be certain as to which of these alternatives is more robust. The power of our study to detect differences in the VDA between study groups was limited by its small sample size. Our recruitment of subjects into this component of the study was limited by resource constraints and, therefore, did not occur consistently throughout the study. The study was not protected by the randomization of the trial design, and there are some differences in the season of recruitment between subjects who provided a breast-milk sample and those who did not provide a breast-milk sample; the proportion of subjects enrolled during the higher-vitamin D-status seasons (summer and fall) compared with the proportion of subjects enrolled during the lower-vitamin D-status seasons (winter and spring) in New Zealand did not differ (52% compared with 49%, respectively; P = 0.68). Adjustments were made in the analyses for the time of year that the samples were taken and the selfreported maternal sunlight exposure. The higher-supplemented group (2000 IU/d) had one more week of gestation than the lower supplemented group (1000 IU/d) had (40 compared with 39 wk, respectively), and this additional week could have potentially contributed to breast-milk VDA being higher in the higher-dose-vitamin D group than in the lower-dose-vitamin D group. Our study design did not allow us to determine the effect of breast-milk VDA on infant serum 25(OH)D concentrations because the infants of mothers who were given vitamin D supplements also were given vitamin D supplements (13) . We enrolled participants from a country where there is very little vitamin D fortification of food, and we achieved high adherence to the assigned study drops. These factors allowed us to show, for the first time to our knowledge, that vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy has potential as a strategy to prevent vitamin deficiency in young breastfed infants, who are a group that have been recognized to be at increased risk of clinical vitamin D deficiency (2).  1 Proportions were compared with the use of the chi-square or Fisher's exact test, and continuous data were compared with the use of an ANOVA or the Wilcoxon's rank-sum test.   Ethnic groups were those used for the national census. M aori is New Zealand's indigenous population. Ethnicity was defined by the participants. An additional strength of our study was the protocol we used for breast-milk sample collection. We were able to collect a full breast-milk sample from each mother, which included including foremilk and hindmilk, and from 44 mothers, we collected breastmilk samples at both 2 wk and 2 mo. Other studies that have described breast-milk VDA either did not report the methods of breast-milk collection or did not report the collection of both foremilk and hindmilk, which is important because most of VDA is in the fat of the hindmilk (11, 19, 20) . Vitamin D supplementation in pregnancy addresses the pregnancy-associated increased maternal requirement for vitamin D and also, as our larger trial showed, prevents the infant from being born in a vitamin D-deficient state (13) . Although this is the first trial, to our knowledge, to investigate the relation of antenatal maternal vitamin D supplementation with breastmilk VDA, there have been 4 published trials of high-dose maternal vitamin D supplementation during lactation in which breast-milk VDA had been measured (11, 12, 21, 22) . These studies were conducted in Finland, the United Arab Emirates, and the United States. In the study by Ala-Houhala et al. (21) in Finland, women supplemented during winter and spring with 1000 or 2000 IU vitamin D 3 /d had higher breast-milk VDA than nonsupplemented mothers did. In this study, the median breastmilk VDA in hindmilk was greater in mothers who, during winter, were supplemented daily with 2000 compared with 1000 IU (VDA: 92 compared with 51 IU/L, respectively). Breast-milk VDA in mothers who took the 2000-IU supplementation in winter remained lower than that in unsupplemented mothers during the summer. The difference in latitude (Tampere: 618N; Auckland: 378S) is one potential explanation for the seasonal effect on breast-milk VDA not being observed in our study. The studies performed in the Unites States showed that the VDA of breast milk can be increased with a maternal vitamin D supplemental dosage .2000 IU/d (11, 12) . In a study in which 64 lactating women were randomly assigned to receive 2000 or 4000 IU/d of a combination of vitamin D 2 and D 3 , the mean breast-milk VDA, which was measured in the 18 women who completed the study to 3 mo postpartum, increased significantly in both groups from 35 to 70 IU/L in the 2000-IU group and from 40 to 135 IU/L in the 4000-IU group. A subsequent study enrolled 19 exclusively breastfeeding women #1 mo postpartum and randomly assigned them to receive 400 or 6400 IU vitamin D 3 /d for 6 mo. Breast-milk VDA of mothers in the 6400-IU/d group increased (82 IU/L at study month 1 and 873 IU/L at study month 7) but varied little in the 400-IU/d group (60 IU/L at study month 1 and 76 IU/L at study month 7). Infant serum 25(OH)D concentrations at age 7 mo were significantly higher (. 100 nmol/L) in both groups than at baseline and did not vary between groups despite that maternal serum 25(OH)D concentrations were significantly higher in mothers assigned to the 6400-IU/d group. In the study conducted in the United Arab Emirates, as a component of a larger trial, breast-milk samples were collected from women assigned at their first postnatal visit to receive 2000 IU vitamin D 2 /d (22) . VDA was undetectable in the 12 samples collected at baseline, and the mean VDA in the 8 samples collected 3 mo after study enrollment was 51 IU/L. A review published in 2012 of the available studies on maternal vitamin D supplementation concluded that, on the basis of the evidence currently available, it is unlikely that maternal vitamin D supplementation during breastfeeding could increase the VDA of breast milk sufficiently to meet infant requirements (8) . A review published in 2013 stated that doses #10 times the current recommended daily intake of vitamin D in lactation are necessary to produce a sufficient transfer of vitamin D to the infant via breast milk (6) . Previous studies have reported that 25 (OH)D is the major circulating form of vitamin D and is poorly transferred into human milk, whereas the parent vitamin D is readily transferred (4, 23) . Thus, vitamin D supplementation of mothers during pregnancy, which achieves maternal serum 25(OH)D concentrations w100 nmol/L at 36 wk of gestation, improves the vitamin D status of the mother, which, in turn, significantly affects the VDA of breast milk (13) In conclusion, the findings from our study indicate that antenatal vitamin D supplementation is a potential option for preventing vitamin D deficiency during early infancy. However, these findings need to be replicated in other studies. Our data suggest an effect that may be achieved with a vitamin D-supplement dosage (i.e., 2000 IU vitamin D 3 /d) that is substantially smaller than the dosages used in the clinical trial studies to date (12) and within the upper limits of safety as recognized by the Institute of Medicine (10) . Although vitamin D supplementation in the current trial of 2000 IU/d did not produce a breast-milk VDA that met the Nutrient Reference Value (Adequate Intake) for infants of 200 IU/d, it provides evidence that vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy increases breast-milk VDA and may be a potentially important public health strategy for supporting maternal postpartum and early infant health.
