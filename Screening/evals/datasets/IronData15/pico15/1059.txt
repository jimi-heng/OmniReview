14-Day High-Dose Amoxicillin- and Metronidazole-Containing Triple Therapy With or Without Bismuth as First-Line Helicobacter pylori Treatment

Laisheng Luo1 · Yingjie Ji1 · Lou Yu1 · Yu Huang1 · Xiao Liang1 · David Y. Graham2 · Hong Lu1,3

Received: 15 September 2019 / Accepted: 27 January 2020 / Published online: 4 February 2020
© Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2020

Abstract
Background Amoxicillin, metronidazole, proton pump inhibitor, bismuth quadruple therapy had been shown to reliably achieve high eradication rates. The role of individual components remains undefined.

Aim To identify the additional benefit/role of bismuth in amoxicillin, metronidazole, proton pump inhibitor, bismuth quadruple therapy for Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) treatment.

Methods This was a non-inferiority factorial design trial. Treatment-naive H. pylori-infected subjects were randomly (1:1) assigned to receive 14-day amoxicillin- and metronidazole-containing triple therapy consisting of esomeprazole 20 mg twice a day, amoxicillin 1 g, and metronidazole 400 mg both thrice daily with or without 220 mg bismuth twice a day. Six weeks after treatment, H. pylori eradication was assessed by $^{13}$C-urea breath test. Antimicrobial susceptibility was assessed by the twofold agar dilution method.

Results From July 2018 to June 2019, a total of two hundred and sixteen subjects were randomized. Both therapies achieved high eradication rates. Per-protocol with bismuth = 97.9% (94/96, 95% CI 95.1–100%) and without bismuth = 94.7% (90/95, 95% CI 90.3–99.1%) ($P = 0.43$). Intent-to-treat analysis = 90.7% (98/108, 95% CI 85.2–96.2%) versus 88.9% (96/108, 95% CI 82.8–95.0%) with and without bismuth ($P = 0.65$). The two regimens were not inferior by intent-to-treat or per-protocol analyses. Metronidazole resistance did not affect the efficacy of either therapy.

Conclusion Neither the presence nor absence of bismuth or metronidazole resistance reduced the effectiveness of triple therapy containing esomeprazole 20 mg twice a day, amoxicillin 1 g, and metronidazole 400 mg thrice daily in this population. The clinical trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03557347.

Keywords Helicobacter pylori · Bismuth · Amoxicillin · Metronidazole · Triple therapy

Introduction

The increased prevalence of antimicrobial resistant Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) has resulted in poor cure rates with most empiric triple therapies and has led to the recommendation to abandon empiric clarithromycin-, metronidazole-, and levofloxacin-containing triple therapies [1]. However, traditional bismuth quadruple therapy (a proton pump inhibitor (PPI), a bismuth, metronidazole, and tetracycline) has remained highly effective especially when administered for 14 days and this regimen is currently recommended as a first choice of empiric regimen (Maastricht, ACG, Toronto, Houston) [1–4]. However, the high frequency of adverse events and often low availability of tetracycline has impeded the universal acceptance of this recommendation [5, 6]. Available alternatives include a variety of modified bismuth quadruple therapies in which tetracycline or metronidazole was successfully replaced by amoxicillin while maintaining high efficacy [6, 7]. Reduction of the metronidazole dosage from 1600 to 1200 mg/d (400 mg, thrice a day) administered with amoxicillin 3 g/d both maintained efficacy with a reduction in adverse events and had a high eradication rate with proven metronidazole-resistant strains (i.e., 97.1%), suggesting that metronidazole was possibly unnecessary [8].

Prior studies had also suggested that increasing the dosage and frequency of antibiotic administration, and the duration of therapy (to 14 days) (e.g., PPI–amoxicillin–metronidazole triple therapy (PAM)) improved results [9]. Here, we compared the efficacy of the high-dose amoxicillin-containing PAM triple therapy with or without bismuth in a non-inferiority study.

**Materials and Methods**

**Study Design and Ethics**

This study was a prospective, open-label, randomized, non-inferiority trial conducted in Renji Hospital affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine in China between July 2018 and June 2019. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Renji Hospital (Approval Number 2018037) and written informed consent were obtained from all the subjects who participated in the trial. The study was also conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The clinical trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03557437. The reporting standards of randomized trials of the CONSORT 2010 statement were followed.

**Subjects and Eligibility Criteria**

Consecutive treatment naïve outpatients with positive $^{13}$C-urea breath tests (UBT) ($>4\%$ cutoff value) who consented to upper gastrointestinal endoscopic examination were assessed for eligibility. $H. pylori$ infection was confirmed by UBT and rapid urease test or histological examination. Exclusion criteria included subjects under 18 or over 80 years old, prior $H. pylori$ treatment history, pregnancy, lactation, gastrectomy, active peptic ulcer, gastrointestinal bleeding, severe systemic diseases, administration of antibiotics, bismuth and acid suppressants in the preceding 12 weeks, and allergy to any one of the used drugs in the regimens.

**Randomization and Blinding**

Eligible subjects were randomly (1:1) assigned to either 14-day PPI, amoxicillin, and metronidazole with or without bismuth by computer-generated random codes with corresponding group assignments kept in sealed opaque envelopes. As this was an open-label study, both the researchers and subjects knew the therapies they received. The staff administering the UBT did not know which treatment the subjects had received.

**Interventions**

Eligible subjects received one of the following two 14-day therapies: esomeprazole (AstraZeneca Pharmaceutical Company, London, England) 20 mg twice daily, amoxicillin (Ruiyang Pharmaceutical, Shandong, China) 1 g, and metronidazole (Xinyi Wanxiang Pharmaceutical Industry, Shanghai, China) 400 mg both thrice daily with bismuth potassium citrate (Dawnrays Pharmaceutical, Jiangsu, China) 600 mg (220 mg elemental bismuth) twice daily (BEAM group) or without bismuth (EAM group). In this study, bismuth and esomeprazole were given 30 min before meals and antibiotics were given 30 min after meals. All subjects were advised to strictly comply with the drug usage, informed about the possible adverse events to improve compliance, and were asked to keep a diary to record adverse events and compliance during the 14-day period. Adverse events were graded as mild (transient and well tolerated), moderate (causing discomfort and partially interfering with daily activities), or severe (causing considerable interference with daily activities). Compliance was defined as good when at least 80% of the total medication had been taken.

Six weeks after completion of the therapy, subjects returned for UBT. Antibiotics, bismuth, and PPI were prohibited in the preceding 4 weeks. Eradication was defined as successful when the UBT result was $<4\%$ (4% as the cutoff value).

**Culture of $H. pylori$ Strains**

Two biopsy specimens with one from the gastric antrum and one from the corpus were collected to isolate $H. pylori$ strains. The biopsy specimens were cultured and maintained on brain heart infusion agar medium (OXOID, Basingstoke, UK) containing 5% defibrinated sheep blood under micro-aerobic conditions (85% $N_2$, 10% $CO_2$, 5% $O_2$) at 37 °C. The isolates were stored in brain heart infusion broth (Difco Laboratory, Detroit, MI, USA) supplemented with 30% glycerol at −80 °C. The isolates were confirmed as $H. pylori$ when urease, oxidase, catalase, and Giemsa staining were positive.

**Determination of Minimal Inhibitory Concentrations**

The minimal inhibitory concentrations of metronidazole, clarithromycin, levofloxacin, and amoxicillin to $H. pylori$ were determined by the twofold agar dilution method. $H. pylori$ was suspended in saline and measured using spectrophotometer. The bacterial suspensions (10^8 colony forming units/ml) were then plated with an inoculator (Sakuma Seisaku, Tokyo, Japan) onto agar plates containing specific

concentrations of the above antibiotics. After 3 days of microaerophilic incubation, minimal inhibitory concentration was defined as the lowest drug concentration that prevented visible growth of bacteria. ATCC43504 was used as the quality control strain [10]. The resistance break points for amoxicillin, metronidazole, clarithromycin, and levofloxacin were defined as 0.125 mg/L, 8 mg/L, 0.5 mg/L, and 1 mg/L, respectively [11].

**Sample Size Estimation and Statistical Analysis**

This was a non-inferiority design trial. The sample size was calculated based on previous similar trials. According to the results of previous studies in our population, the eradication rate of triple therapy containing esomeprazole, amoxicillin, and tinidazole was 92.3% [12], and the eradication rate of modified bismuth quadruple therapy was 96.9% [7]. Assuming 92.3% eradication rate in the EAM group, 96.9% eradication rate in the BEAM group, a non-inferiority margin of –10% [13], an α-error of 0.025 (one-side), a power of 90%, and a ratio of 1:1, at least 96 subjects in each group would be required to show non-inferiority of two groups on the basis of $H. pylori$ eradication rate in this trial. Considering that 10% of subjects may be lost to follow-up, at least 212 subjects in total were expected to be recruited in the trial.

Intention-to-treat (ITT), per-protocol (PP), and modified ITT (MITT) analyses were used to evaluate the $H. pylori$ eradication rates of each group. Subjects who took at least one dose of the medication and performed UBT for reexamining the $H. pylori$ status were included in the MITT analysis. Comparative non-inferiority of the two groups was assessed through derivation of a two-sided 95% confidence interval (CI). If the lower bound of the 95% CI of the difference between two groups exceeded -10%, non-inferiority of EAM triple therapy vs. BEAM quadruple therapy could be concluded. Subgroup analyses were also performed based on antimicrobial resistance data. Between-group differences were evaluated using Student’s $t$ test for continuous variables and Pearson’s $\chi^2$ test or Chi-square with Yates’s correction for categorical variables, as appropriate. The statistical significance level was set at $P < 0.05$.

**Results**

The flow of screening and recruitment of study subjects is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 216 subjects were recruited and randomly assigned to EAM or BEAM groups with 108 subjects in each group included in ITT analysis. Five subjects in the EAM group and seven subjects in the BEAM group were lost to follow-up and did not return for UBT 6 weeks after completion of therapy. These twelve subjects were scored as treatment failures in the ITT analysis. Furthermore, eight subjects and five subjects in EAM group and BEAM group, respectively, failed to take at least 80% of the drugs but received follow-up UBT. These 25 subjects (13 in EAM group and 12 in BEAM group) were excluded from the PP analysis but included in the MITT analyses as only subjects lost to follow-up (without UBT results) were excluded from the MITT analysis.

H. pylori Eradication Rates

As shown in Table 2, the eradication rates by ITT and PP analysis were 88.9% (96/108, 95% CI 82.8–95.0%) and 94.7% (90/95, 95% CI 90.3–99.1%) in the EAM group and 90.7% (98/108, 95% CI 85.2–96.2%) and 97.9% (94/96, 95% CI 95.1–100%) in the BEAM group, respectively. In MITT analysis, the eradication rate was 93.2% (96/103, 95% CI 88.4–98.0%) with six subjects with negative UBT results of eight subjects of low compliance in the EAM group, versus 97.0% (98/101, 95% CI 93.7–100%) with four subjects with negative UBT results of five subjects of low compliance in the BEAM group.

The difference of eradication rates between EAM and BEAM groups was -1.8%, -3.2%, and -3.8%, respectively, and was not statistically significant by ITT, PP, and MITT analysis ($P=0.65, 0.43$ and 0.21). What is more, the lower bound of the 95% CI for the difference of the eradication rates from BEAM therapy in ITT ($-9.8–6.2\%$, PP $(-8.5–2.1\%$), and MITT $(-9.6–2.0\%)$ analysis was greater than the pre-specified non-inferiority margin of -10%. EAM triple therapy fulfilled the criteria for non-inferiority to BEAM quadruple therapy in the ITT, PP, and MITT analysis.

Among smokers, there was no significant difference of eradication rates between EAM and BEAM groups by ITT analysis (70.6%, 12/17 vs. 75.0%, 12/16, $P=1.0$) and by PP analysis (84.6%, 11/13 vs. 92.3%, 12/13, $P=1.0$) as shown in Table S1. In this study, adding bismuth showed no benefit among smokers.

Susceptibility Testing

Two hundred and seven strains with 105 (97.2%, 105/108) in the EAM group and 102 (94.4%, 102/108) in the BEAM group were successfully cultured and recovered from 216 subjects. The resistance rate of H. pylori for amoxicillin was 0.9%(2/207), 75.8%(157/207) for metronidazole, 30.9% (64/207) for clarithromycin, and 42.0% (87/207) for levofloxacin (Table 1).

In order to determine the effects of metronidazole resistance on *H. pylori* eradication rates in the PP population, subgroup analyses were also performed. As shown in Table 3, there was no significant difference of eradication rates between those who did or did not receive bismuth co-therapy (93.2 vs. 96.9%, \( P = 0.54 \)) among those with metronidazole-resistant infections. Table 3 shows the eradication rates with metronidazole-susceptible and-resistant strains with EAM therapy (susceptible 100%, 19/19, and resistant 93.2%, 68/73, \( P = 0.55 \)) and with BEAM therapy (susceptible 100%, 26/26, and resistant 96.9%, 63/65, \( P = 1.00 \)).

### Adverse Events and Compliance

As shown in Table 4, adverse events were reported in 42.5% (46/108) in the EAM group versus 39.8% (43/108) (\( P = 0.68 \)) in the BEAM group. Nausea, dizziness, abdominal discomfort, and diarrhea were the most common adverse events. All adverse events disappeared after cessation of treatment. Six subjects in EAM group (5.6%) and five subjects in BEAM group (4.6%) discontinued drugs due to adverse events (\( P = 0.76 \)). Overall, mild and moderate adverse events accounted for 95.7% and 100% of total adverse events in EAM group and BEAM group with only two severe adverse events (one was skin rash and one was headache) in the EAM group. Compliance was good in both EAM and BEAM groups (92.6% vs. 95.4%, \( P = 0.39 \)).

### Discussion

Early in the history of *H. pylori* therapy, PPI, amoxicillin, and metronidazole triple therapy was used successfully but was shown to be influenced by both antibiotic dose and duration of therapy [14, 15]. Overall, metronidazole triple therapy was not recommended as a first-line empiric therapy as it had relative low efficacy in many western countries.

due to resistance and the adverse effects of metronidazole resistance on outcome [9]. In 2016, Zhou et al. [12] gave amoxicillin 1 g and tinidazole 0.5 g both three times a day for 10 days and reported a PP eradication rate of 92.7% with an eradication rate of 90.4% for metronidazole-resistant strains versus 95.3% with susceptible strains. The present study compared 14-day triple therapy containing amoxicillin 1 g and metronidazole 400 mg both thrice daily with the corresponding bismuth quadruple therapy both using the same doses of amoxicillin and metronidazole as this bismuth quadruple therapy had been used in our previously reported first-line treatments [8]. Both the triple therapy and the corresponding bismuth quadruple therapy provided excellent results with eradication rates of 94.7% and 97.9% by PP analysis, and with eradication rates of 93.2% and 96.9% with metronidazole-resistant strains, respectively. The triple therapy proved to be not inferior to the corresponding bismuth quadruple therapy.

These data suggested that increasing the dosage of amoxicillin to 3 g/d (1 g thrice daily) and metronidazole to 1200 mg/d (400 mg thrice daily) as well as the duration of therapy to 14 days could overcome high metronidazole resistance and improve cure rates. This finding was also consistent with, and helped clarify prior studies, suggesting that metronidazole resistance could be partially or completely overcome by increasing the dose and duration of therapy [7, 8, 16]. However, the mechanism of overcoming metronidazole resistance remains unknown and this study shows that the phenomena would be more accurately described as improving the outcome in the presence of metronidazole-resistant infections rather than as overcoming metronidazole resistance.

Amoxicillin remains a very important antibiotic used in H. pylori treatment as it is effective and resistance remains low. In this study, H. pylori resistance to amoxicillin was 0.9% which was consistent with previous reports [7, 17]. The bactericidal effect of amoxicillin is thought to in part hinge on the percent time above the MIC rather than the maximum plasma concentration [18]. Our experience with dual high-dose PPI–amoxicillin therapy in this population suggests that the high cure rates achieved with the triple therapy can largely be attributed to the high-dose amoxicillin dual component [19, 20]. A meta-analysis including ninety-four studies (8061 patients) comparing PPI–amoxicillin–metronidazole (PAM) therapies versus PPI–amoxicillin–clarithromycin therapies indicated that results of PAM triple therapies containing low doses of amoxicillin were inferior to PAM triple therapies containing high doses of amoxicillin but with same dose of metronidazole, suggesting that amoxicillin played a major role in PAM triple therapies [9, 21–26]. In retrospect, knowing the results of this experiment, it appears that inclusion of a PPI–amoxicillin arm would have been extremely helpful in assisting in explaining the results of the present study. The effectiveness of high-dose PPI–amoxicillin dual therapy has differed in different areas [27]. In our region of China, dual therapy has proven highly effective and we may recommend it as a first-line therapy [19, 20]. This is also based on the premise that only proven locally highly effective regimens should be used. But lacking a head-to-head comparison between dual therapy and triple therapy makes it impossible to make a recommendation concerning whether it is possible to eliminate metronidazole. The effectiveness of the dual or triple therapy needs further confirmation in other countries and populations.

In classic bismuth quadruple therapy, it is possible that the ability to overcome metronidazole resistance is largely due to the additive effect of bismuth [28, 29]. Both bismuth and high-dose amoxicillin can both independently theoretically improve treatment results [19, 20]. We also used a relative low dose of PPI (esomeprazole 20 mg/dose equivalent to 32 mg of omeprazole) which may have partially reduced the effectiveness of the 3 g amoxicillin component, and subsequent studies with higher doses of PPI are also needed.

Adverse events were relatively frequent (42.5% vs. 39.8%); however, compliance was good (92.6% vs. 95.4%) in both treatment groups. It is reasonable to consider more drugs and more complicated of combination in a therapy may contribute to higher adverse events and lower compliance in real world. Simpler but equally effective combinations like triple and dual therapies could improve compliance and consequently cure rates.

There were some limitations in our study. First, this was an open label rather than a double-blind trial such that both the doctors and subjects knew which medications they received, which may influence the compliance of patients and outcomes of this study. Second, this was a single-center study and sample size was relatively small. Results obtained in this study should be confirmed by multicenter trial and in larger populations. Third, the conclusion derived from this study could not be applied to subjects allergic to penicillin. Last, we did not simultaneously compare the efficacy of corresponding dual therapy with triple therapy in this study.

In summary, our results showed that in our population 14 days with a PPI plus high-dose amoxicillin and metronidazole with or without bismuth achieved excellent eradication rates and were well tolerated despite most patients had metronidazole-resistant infections.
