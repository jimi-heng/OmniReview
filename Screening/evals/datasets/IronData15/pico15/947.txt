Two-Day Quadruple Therapy for Cure of Helicobacter pylori Infection: A Comparative, Randomized Trial

X. Calvet, M.D., N. García, M.D., R. Campo, M.D., E. Brullet, M.D., R. Comet, M.D., and M. Navarro, M.D.
Internal Medicine and Surgery departments, and Digestive Endoscopy Unit (SDI),
Consorci Hospitalari del Parc Taulí, Sabadell, Spain

Objective: We sought to compare a 2-day quadruple therapy with a 14-day triple therapy in the treatment of Helicobacter pylori infection. Methods: Eighty-one consecutive patients with an endoscopically diagnosed peptic ulcer and demonstrated infection by H. pylori were included in the study. Patients were randomized to receive omeprazole 40 mg bid., amoxicillin 2.5 g once daily, metronidazole 500 mg tid., and bismuth subcitrate 360 mg tid. for 2 days, followed by omeprazole 20 mg once daily for 6 additional days (Group 1) or a 14-day course of omeprazole 20 mg bid., amoxicillin 1 g tid., and metronidazole 500 mg tid. (Group 2). Eradication was evaluated by antral biopsy and rapid urease test at 2 months after therapy and by C13.

and metronidazole 500 mg t.i.d. Ranitidine 300 mg daily was indicated after treatment in patients with prior history of bleeding peptic ulcer until cure of H. pylori infection was confirmed. Proton pump inhibitors and antibiotics were not allowed after treatment. Compliance and side effects were assessed by structural clinical interview at the first follow-up control after treatment. All but two patients (one in each group) returned for control. Gastroscopy with antral biopsies for histological study and rapid urease test were performed between 8 and 16 wk after the end of eradication therapy. When a discordance between histology and the rapid urease test was observed, a $^{13}$C-urea breath test was performed and, if negative, the infection was considered cured. A $^{13}$C-urea breath test was performed 12 months after treatment in 55 previously cured patients to confirm the clearance of H. pylori infection. Patients were also asked about the presence of symptoms suggesting peptic ulcer disease during the previous year. Nine of the patients who did not return for the 1-yr follow-up control were contacted by telephone to assess the presence of clinical symptoms and the need for antilcer therapy after the eradication treatment.

**Statistical methods**

Comparison of proportions was performed using the $\chi^2$ test; the Mann-Whitney test was used for comparison of quantitative variables. Data are given as mean $\pm$ SD. Calculations were performed using the SPSS for Windows statistical package.

**Sample size calculation.** For a cure rate of 95% of the control group, a sample size of 60 patients per group was calculated to exclude a difference between groups $> 15\%$ with an $\alpha$ risk of 0.05 and a power of 0.8. An interim analysis was planned when data from the 60 first patients were available, to avoid treating a large group of patients if 2-day therapy achieved very low rates of eradication.

**RESULTS**

The results of the interim analysis of the first 60 patients showed a very significant difference in the cure rates between the two groups; the study was subsequently stopped. By then, 81 patients had entered the study. Forty-two received 2-day therapy and 39 received 14-day therapy. Patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the two groups and are shown in Table 1.

**Cure of H. pylori infection**

Intention-to-treat analysis showed that H. pylori infection was cured in 29 of 42 patients (69%; 95% CI: 53–82%) in Group 1 versus 36 of 39 (92%; 95% CI: 78–98%) of patients in Group 2 ($p = 0.009$). Per-protocol analysis showed a cure rate of 71% (95% CI: 55–84%; 29/41 patients) and 95% (95% CI: 81–99%; 36/38 patients), respectively ($p = 0.007$).

Fifty-five of 65 cured patients returned for control 1 yr after treatment (26 in Group 1, 29 in Group 2). All these patients were free of ulcer symptoms without treatment, and $C^{13}$-urea breath test was negative in all but one patient in Group 2. Nine of the 10 patients who did not return for the 1-yr follow-up control were interviewed by phone in February 1997; all denied ulcer symptoms and none of them had required further study or treatment for peptic ulcer disease.

**Compliance with treatment**

Differences in treatment compliance were not significant, although it was slightly lower in the 2-day treatment group; 88.1% of patients in Group 1 (37/42 patients) versus 92.3% in Group 2 (36/39 patients) had taken all the pills prescribed.

**Incidence of side effects**

Side effects were observed in 27% (95% CI: 15–43%) of the patients in Group 1 and in 41% (95% CI: 26–58%) in Group 2. The difference did not reach statistical significance. Interestingly, severe side effects were more frequent in the 14-day therapy group although the difference again did not reach statistical significance. Severe glossitis and pharyngitis with erythema and odynophagia developed in three patients in Group 2 and in none in Group 1. Symptoms usually disappeared in a few days, but in one patient symptoms lasted for as long as 2 wk after the end of therapy. By contrast, only one patient in the 2-day therapy presented a bout of bloody diarrhea, which spontaneously subsided after the end of therapy.

**DISCUSSION**

In our study, 2-day treatment cured 69% of patients with H. pylori infection. This result is similar to those of recent

trials dealing with 2-day therapy (9, 10). Kung et al. (9) reported an intention-to-treat cure rate of 76% (35 of 46 patients) with a 2-day combination of omeprazole 20 mg b.i.d. and tetracycline 500 mg, metronidazole 400 mg, and bismuth subcitrate 240 mg q.i.d., results that are in the range of the present report. Tucci et al. (10) have reported better results, with a 90% cure rate in 30 patients using a treatment very similar to ours. However, these results cannot be applied to routine clinical practice because patients with pretreatment resistance to tinidazole were excluded. It is unlikely that a therapy that requires pretreatment sensitivity testing will ever be applied in clinical practice.

In the present study we used a high dose of amoxicillin once a day (instead of the usual b.i.d. or t.i.d. schedules) and bismuth subcitrate t.i.d. (instead of the usual q.i.d. schedule) in an effort to simplify the eradication therapy. In general, q.i.d. and even t.i.d. schedules are impractical and induce a notably high rate of noncompliance. This is doubtless underestimated in published H. pylori treatment studies, which tend to deal with highly motivated and informed practitioners and patients. This may account for the fact that in our study the rate of noncompliance was relatively low and similar between the two groups, despite their difference in the number of days and daily doses.

It appears, then, that the ideal standard treatment should use a twice-a-day or even a once-a-day schedule; indeed, one of the most frequent treatments today, the Bazzoli 7-day triple therapy (13), uses a b.i.d. schedule for all drugs in the regimen.

We should also stress that the reduction of the number of daily doses in our study did not decrease the cure rate in comparison with other 2-day studies; however, further investigations will be necessary to evaluate whether simplified therapies are feasible for H. pylori treatment.

The present study was designed in 1993. At that time, the only information available on very short eradication treatment was the study of Tucci et al. (8), who obtained an eradication rate of 72% with only 1 day of therapy. Therefore, we expected a significant increase in the eradication rate by doubling the duration of treatment. In addition, when the study was designed, a 14-day therapy with amoxicillin, metronidazole, and omeprazole was a reasonable choice as control treatment. In fact, we found very high rates of cure of the infection, and this therapy should, therefore, be considered as a valid alternative for treatment. One possible explanation for this excellent response to triple therapy with metronidazole is that the prevalence of metronidazole resistance in our study population was low. Although we did not test for pretreatment sensitivity to metronidazole, this explanation is in fact highly unlikely, as the prevalence of resistance found in a study in another hospital in our area with a similar population was high (15–20%) (14) and because metronidazole resistance has been reported to be uniformly high all over Europe (15, 16).

Adding our results to the other 2-day series available it seems that 2-day therapies are unlikely to reach the 90% cure rate required for acceptance as a valuable tool in clinical practice (3). Therefore, unless more effective drugs are developed in the future, therapies lasting longer than 2 days should be considered. Pilot studies with 4-day quadruple therapy have shown promising results (6, 7), but whether 4, 7, 10, or even 14 days will become the standard remains an open question and only large multicenter studies will answer it (1).
