Optimizing first line 7-day standard triple therapy for Helicobacter pylori eradication: Prolonging treatment or adding bismuth: which is better?

Objective: The 7-day standard triple therapy (STT) gives unacceptably low eradication rates of Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori). We aimed to examine whether extending STT from 7 days to 14 days or adding a bismuth compound to a 7-day STT would result in better eradication rates.

Methods: H. pylori-positive patients were assigned to Group A (7-day STT; rabeprazole 20 mg twice daily, amoxicillin 1 g twice daily, and clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, for 7 days), Group B (7-day STT with bismuth; rabeprazole 20 mg twice daily, amoxicillin 1 g twice daily, clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily and bismuth subcitrate 240 mg twice daily, for 7 days) and Group C (14-day STT; rabeprazole 20 mg twice daily, amoxicillin 1 g twice daily, and clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily for 14 days). Eradication was tested using ¹³C-UBT at least 4 weeks after the completion of therapy.

Results: A total of 364 patients were recruited. In the intention-to-treat analysis, eradication rates were 79.3% (96/121; 95% confidence interval [CI] 71.3–85.6%) for 7-day STT, 81.7% (98/120; 95% CI 73.8–87.6%) for 7-day STT with bismuth, and 88.6% (109/123; 95% CI 81.8–93.1%) for 14-day STT, respectively. Statistical significance was achieved between the 7-day and the 14-day STT treatment (P = 0.048).

Conclusions: Adding bismuth to the 7-day STT did not result in an increase in the eradication rate. Extending the STT to 14 days, however, achieved a significantly higher eradication rate. Nevertheless, this did not achieve the targeted 90% eradication rate on intention-to-treat analysis.

KEYWORDS
bismuth, Helicobacter pylori eradication, proton pump inhibitors, standard triple therapy

1 | INTRODUCTION

The 7-day standard triple therapy (STT) with proton pump inhibitor (PPI) and two antibiotics—amoxicillin and clarithromycin—has been previously recommended as first-line treatment for eradicating Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection by various consensus guidelines.¹–³ However, the eradication success has decreased over the years. Although reported eradication rates vary with this treatment regimen, rates of less than 80% on intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis are nowadays typical.⁴

The 7-day STT was first widely adopted in clinical practice in the late 1990s and was found to be efficacious, convenient, and tolerable, ensuring a high compliance from patients.⁵,⁶ But with the increasing use of antibiotics, bacterial resistance, especially to clarithromycin, has increased across the world.⁷–⁹ This has been thought to be the primary reason why treatment success with the 7-day STT has now decreased significantly.⁴ The Maastricht V consensus guidelines have again highlighted this problem and recommended that clarithromycin

should not be used in regions where the resistance rates exceeds 15%. In line with the treatment of all other infectious diseases, Graham et al. have proposed that a higher eradication success target rate of at least 90% should be adopted. In Malaysia we have shown that the STT for 7 days has not been effective and has an ITT eradication rate of only 71.2%. Clearly, recommended treatment regimens have to change. Although in clinical practice sequential and concomitant therapies have been used, they have not been popular with practicing clinicians in Malaysia as they have found these regimens complex and patients have difficulty in complying with them.

In this study, we sought to determine whether extending the STT from 7 days to 14 days or adding a bismuth compound to the 7-day STT would result in a better eradication rates than the 7-day STT in our local population.

2 | PATIENTS AND METHODS 

2.1 | Selection of the patients

Consecutive patients diagnosed with H. pylori infection for the first time, based on a positive rapid urease test (RUT), were recruited for the study. All these patients had undergone an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy for dyspepsia at the Outpatient Endoscopy Unit, University of Malaya Medical Centre (Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia) from January 2013 to December 2015.

Dyspepsia was defined as persistent or recurrent upper abdominal pain or discomfort over the preceding 3 months. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) pregnant or breastfeeding individuals; (ii) patients with significant concomitant medical illnesses; (iii) with a previous history of gastrointestinal surgery; or (iv) having known allergies to any component of the treatment regimen, especially to penicillin. Only patients who were receiving H. pylori treatment for the first time were included. Those with treatment failures were excluded. Following endoscopy, all participants were categorized into four groups as follows: gastric ulcer, duodenal ulcer, both gastric and duodenal ulcers, and non-ulcer dyspepsia. Patients with complicated ulcers were also excluded from the study.

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before they were included in the study. The study protocol, patient’s information sheet and consent form were approved by the University of Malaya Medical Centre Ethics Committee and were performed according to International Conference on Harmonization—Good Clinical Practice (GCP—ICHC) guidelines.

2.2 | Study design and treatment

This study was a comparative, randomized, open-label study. At gastroscopy, at least two biopsy specimens from the gastric antrum and two from the gastric body were taken from each participant and tested with an RUT. The test result was regarded as positive when the urea solution had turned unequivocally red. This test has been previously validated in our local population. All participants were randomly assigned to one of the three treatment groups: Group A (7-day STT): rabeprazole (Pariet, Eisai, Tokyo, Japan) 20 mg twice daily, amoxicillin (Ospamox, Biochemie, Kundl, Austria) 1 g twice daily and clarithromycin (Klaid, Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, USA) 500 mg twice daily, for 7 days; Group B (7-day STT with bismuth): rabeprazole (Pariet, Eisai) 20 mg twice daily, amoxicillin (Ospamox, Biochemie) 1 g twice daily, bismuth subcitrate (De-Nol tab, Astellas Pharma Ltd, Chertsey, UK) 240 mg twice daily and clarithromycin (Abbott Laboratories) 500 mg twice daily, for 7 days; and Group C (14-day STT): rabeprazole (Pariet, Eisai) 20 mg twice daily, amoxicillin (Ospamox, Biochemie) 1 g twice daily and clarithromycin (Abbott Laboratories) 500 mg twice daily, for 14 days.

All participants were informed about the potential side effects of the medications and the importance of completing the entire course of the treatment. They were asked to return at one or two weeks after completing the therapy in order to check for their compliance, which was defined as completing at least 85% of the prescribed medications, and to report any side effects experienced.

2.3 | Sample size calculation

Based on an estimate of a 15% difference in eradication rates of H. pylori with a power of 80% and two-sided significance level of 95%, a sample size of 110 per arm was computed.

2.4 | Determination of successful H. pylori eradication

All participants underwent a clinic follow-up and a 13C-urea breath test (UBT; IRIS, Wagner Analysen Technik™, Worpswede, Germany) at least 4 weeks after the completion of therapy. Successful eradication of H. pylori was defined as a negative 13C-UBT.

Eradication rates were calculated as for ITT and per-protocol (PP) analyses with 95% confidence interval (CI). We set a target of a 90% eradication rate, based on ITT analysis as an acceptable treatment level of treatment success (grade B, based on Graham’s report card).

2.5 | Statistical analysis

All data were entered into a SPSS program version 15 (Armonk, NY, USA) for statistical analyses. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or medians and ranges, and the categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. The Fisher’s exact test and $\chi^2$ test were used to compare the data among the groups. A two-tailed test was used in all analyses and a P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3.1 | Treatment success of H. pylori eradication

The ITT analysis showed that H. pylori was eradicated in 79.3% (96/121) of the patients in Group A (95% CI 71.3–85.6%), 81.7% (98/120; 95% CI 73.8–87.6%) of those in Group B and 88.6% (109/123; 95% CI 81.8–93.1%) of those in group C (Table 1). Comparing the groups based on the ITT analysis, the P values were 0.648 for Group B vs Group A, 0.048 for Group C vs Group A, and 0.127 for Group C vs Group B.

PP analysis showed that the eradication rates were 82.8% (96/116) of patients in Group A (95% CI 74.9–88.6%), 85.2% (98/115; 95% CI 77.6–90.6%) in Group B, and 91.6% (109/119; 95% CI 85.2–95.4%) in Group C, respectively. Comparing the groups in the PP analysis, P values were 0.610 when comparing Group B with Group A, 0.042 for Group C vs Group A, and 0.127 for Group C vs Group B.

There were few patients with peptic ulcers. There were no significant difference in eradication rates of H. pylori in participants with ulcers compared with the non-ulcer dyspepsia group (P = 1.000). Similarly, there were no significant differences in eradication rates between male and female participants (P = 0.380).

3.2 | Side effects and tolerability of treatment

The treatment regimens were generally well tolerated. There were no significant differences in adverse events between all three treatment groups. In Group A, 17 (14.0%) complained of altered taste (dysgeusia), five (4.1%) patients of diarrhea, 8 (6.6%) of abdominal pain and three (2.5%) of skin rash. The major complaint of patients in Group B was black stools—18 (15.0%), 10 (8.3%) of altered taste and with a few patients complaining of abdominal pain and diarrhea. In Group C, 23 (18.7%) patients complained of altered taste and seven (5.7%) patients each complained of diarrhea and abdominal pain. All side effects were reported as mild. 4 | DISCUSSION

Treatment success in all groups did not meet our target of 90% eradication rate on ITT analysis. Although the rate was numerically higher, adding bismuth to the STT did not reach statistical significance compared with the 7-day STT. Prolonging STT from 7 days to 14 days resulted in a significantly higher eradication rate but this was also below 90% (88.6%). Our results were not confounded by weight and the proportion of patients who were smokers which was similar across all three treatment groups. There was also no difference in the ratio between patients with peptic ulcers and non-ulcer dyspepsia.

As in many countries throughout the world, the 7-day STT has been the recommended treatment regimen in Malaysia for many years\(^14\) and is widely prescribed. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was not performed in our study. However, based on reports of very low or zero clarithromycin resistance in our local population\(^15\) we have continued to use clarithromycin in our treatment regimens.

Extending the duration of therapy has been shown in a worldwide systematic analysis to result in a significant increase in eradication rate.\(^16\) We have shown in our study that treatment success was significantly increased compared with the 7-day STT. However, we had hoped that the 14-day STT would have achieved our target eradication rate of 90%, but our final result fell short of this. H. pylori has no resistance to bismuth compounds. In our other treatment arm, we added bismuth in standard doses to the 7-day STT. However, this did not achieve a better eradication rate than the 7-day STT either.

Graham and Dore have strongly recommended susceptibility testing for all patients given H. pylori eradication therapy.\(^17\) While testing for bacterial resistance, including molecular testing for clarithromycin resistance have been carried out in Malaysia, this is still not widely available. We have based our choice of antibiotics on our local resistance patterns. No resistance to amoxicillin has been reported.\(^15\) Resistance to clarithromycin has consistently been low in Malaysia. Rates of 1.8%, 0%, 6.8% and 1.2%, in four studies have been reported.\(^15,18–20\) These studies have been conducted in the same locality as our study and it is therefore perplexing that we have not

been able to achieve a higher eradication rate with our 14-day therapy. We were not able to carry out susceptibility testing on bacterial strains in our patients in this study.

There was a lack of improvement in treatment success with the addition of bismuth in our study. In a previous study by Sun et al. an eradication rate on ITT analysis of only 80% was achieved with a 7-day STT plus bismuth. However, when this therapy was extended to 14 days, the eradication rate rose to 93.7% on ITT (P = 0.01). Regrettably, in our study we did not include an additional arm of STT plus bismuth that was extending to 14 days.

In a recent study by Long et al. bismuth increased the eradication rate substantially in a 14-day PPI-containing triple therapy with clarithromycin and metronidazole. In this pilot study, metronidazole was used in a four-time daily dose and a high dose of bismuth of 600 mg twice daily, with a high rate of adverse events reported.

Based on our studies, better treatment regimens should be tested and used to achieve an eradication rate of 90% or more on ITT analysis.
