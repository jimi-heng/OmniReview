Failure of a 1-day high-dose quadruple therapy for cure of Helicobacter pylori infection

J. WERMEILLE*, M. CUNNINGHAM†, B. ARMENIAN†, G. ZELGER*, P. BURI‡, H. MERKI§ & A. HADENGUE†
*Department of Pharmacology and Anaesthesiology, University Hospitals of Geneva; †Division of Gastroenterology and Hepatology, Department of Internal Medicine, University Hospitals of Geneva; ‡Pharmacy Section, Faculty of Sciences, University of Geneva; and §Department of Gastroenterology, Beau-site Hospitals Bern, Switzerland

Accepted for publication 20 September 1998

SUMMARY

Background: The optimal duration of treatment for eradication of Helicobacter pylori has still to be defined. A 1-day high-dose quadruple therapy with a combination of amoxycillin (or tetracycline), metronidazole, a bismuth salt and a proton pump inhibitor has led to eradication rates of 57–77%. In view of the high frequency of metronidazole-resistant strains of H. pylori in Europe, we hypothesized that by using clarithromycin in place of metronidazole and by increasing the dose of proton pump inhibitor, the efficacy of a 1-day high-dose quadruple therapy could be improved.

Methods: Patients were randomized to receive either amoxycillin 1000 mg b.d., clarithromycin 500 mg b.d. and lansoprazole 30 mg b.d. for 7 days, or amoxycillin 2000 mg q.d.s., clarithromycin 500 mg q.d.s., lansoprazole 30 mg t.d.s. and bismuth subcitrate 240 mg q.d.s. for 1 day.

Results: It was originally intended to include 100 patients. The first planned interim analysis performed after follow-up was completed for 30 patients revealed H. pylori eradication rates of 80% (12/15) in the 7-day triple therapy group and 20% (3/15) in the 1-day quadruple therapy group, the difference being highly significant (P = 0.003). Because the efficacy of the 1-day treatment was so low, the study was stopped for ethical reasons. Eleven patients who failed with the 1-day treatment were re-treated with the 7-day triple therapy: the eradication rate was 91% (10/11).

Conclusions: One-day high-dose quadruple therapy with amoxycillin, clarithromycin, lansoprazole and bismuth subcitrate is dramatically less effective than the classic 7-day triple therapy with the same antibiotics.

INTRODUCTION

There is at present clear evidence that Helicobacter pylori is the main aetiologic agent of most gastritis (type B gastritis) and peptic ulceration. Eradication of infection leads to resolution of the gastritis and to spectacular decreases of duodenal and gastric ulcer relapses.1–6 H. pylori therapies are usually given for 7 days and include two antibiotics and either a proton pump inhibitor or a bismuth salt.7–10 These types of therapy generally achieve about 90% eradication. However, the large doses of drugs and numerous side-effects contribute to poor patient compliance and reduce treatment efficacy.11,12 Availability of an ultra-short therapy of 1 day with the same efficacy would therefore be a significant advantage. Tucci et al.,13 De Boer et al.,14 Takats et al.15 and Dobrucali et al.16 obtained eradication rates of 72%, 57%, 77% and 73%, respectively, with 1-day quadruple therapies including amoxycillin (or tetracycline14), metronidazole, a bismuth salt and a proton pump inhibitor (omeprazole or lansoprazole).

In the present study, we investigated the possibility of obtaining comparable efficacy for a 1-day quadruple therapy and a standard 7-day triple therapy which is reported to achieve 90% eradication. Clarithromycin was used because the prevalence of resistant H. pylori strains in Europe is known to be lower for clarithromycin (3–10%) than for metronidazole (10–45%). We also increased the dose of the proton pump inhibitor, to potentiate the effects of amoxycillin and clarithromycin. Proton pump inhibitors are known to increase the intragastric concentrations of these antibiotics; efficacy is best when intragastric pH approaches 7.23–26.

**MATERIALS AND METHODS**

**Patients and diagnosis**

Patients with endoscopically proven gastric or duodenal ulcer, erosive gastritis or duodenitis entered the study between May 1996 and January 1997. H. pylori infection was diagnosed using the rapid urease test. From two biopsies (one from the antrum and one from the corpus). Exclusion criteria were: (i) patients aged < 20 years; (ii) history of allergy to any of the drugs used in the study; (iii) major organic, systemic, metabolic or psychiatric diseases; (iv) pregnancy; (v) patients having received antibiotics (other than those proposed for eradication of H. pylori) during the study period. All patients included in the study were receiving an eradication treatment of H. pylori for the first time and none had taken an antisecretory agent (proton pump inhibitor or H2-receptor antagonist) or an antibiotic during the week before the therapy. The study was approved by the ethical committee of the University Hospitals of Geneva, and all patients gave written informed consent. Endoscopy was performed with Olympus (GIF-100) or Pentax (EG-2901) gastroscopes. Endoscopes and biopsy equipment were disinfected between the procedures with 2% glutaraldehyde, according to standard procedures.

**Treatment**

The patients were randomized to either 7-day triple therapy comprising lansoprazole 30 mg b.d., amoxycillin 1000 mg b.d., clarithromycin 500 mg b.d., with meals; or a 1-day quadruple therapy of lansoprazole 30 mg t.d.s., amoxycillin 2000 mg q.d.s., clarithromycin 500 mg q.d.s., with meals, and bismuth subcitrate 240 mg q.d.s., 30 min before meals.

**Assessment of side-effects and patient compliance**

All patients were seen 1–4 days after the end of the therapy and interviewed about possible side-effects and compliance. Pill counts were performed.

**Assessment of eradication**

H. pylori status was determined non-invasively 6–8 weeks after treatment by ¹³C-urea breath test, performed according to the European protocol (positive = excess δ¹³C > 5/mL) (specificity and sensibility 95–100%). A positive test indicates treatment failure.

**Statistical methods**

Assuming a mean eradication rate of 90% for the 7-day triple therapy, statistical theory shows that the null hypothesis (same efficacy for both treatments) can only be rejected at the 5% significance level if the success rate of the experimental treatment is lower than 70%, when the sample size is 49 patients in each group (β = 20%). It was therefore intended to include 50 patients in each group. However, for ethical reasons, periodical interim analyses were scheduled after consecutive recruitment of 30 and 60 patients, so that recruitment could be interrupted if a dramatically poor efficacy of the experimental treatment was found. The difference between mean ages was evaluated with Student’s t-test. The association between categorical variables was assessed by chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. The null hypothesis of no difference was rejected at a P-value of < 0.05. The binomial distribution was used to determine the exact value of the 95% confidence interval.

**RESULTS**

The planned interim analysis, performed when the first 30 patients had completed the study, revealed a dramatically lower rate of eradication in the 1-day treatment group; further recruitment was therefore stopped.

Fifteen patients were treated with the 1-day quadruple therapy and 19 with the 7-day triple therapy (group A). In addition, 11 patients were treated with the 7-day triple therapy (group B) after failure of the 1-day treatment. Four patients were lost to follow-up (all in the 7-day therapy group A) and did not return for a $^{13}$C-urea breath test. One of these discontinued his treatment after experiencing severe headache, nausea and vomiting, 15 min after the first intake of each drug (amoxycillin, clarithromycin and lansoprazole). The causality of the drugs is, however, not clearly established as this patient was known to suffer from severe headaches since childhood.

Table 2 shows that by a per protocol analysis (including only patients effectively submitted to the $^{13}$C-urea breath test 6–8 weeks after completion of the therapy), the $H. pylori$ eradication rate of the 1-day therapy group (20%, 95% CI: 4.3–48.1%) differed significantly from the rate for the 7-day therapy group A (80%, 95% CI: 51.9–95.7%) and from the 7-day group B (91%) ($P = 0.003$ and 0.001, respectively). On intention-to-treat analysis (including the four patients not evaluated), the eradication rate was also significantly lower in the 1-day therapy group (20%, 95% CI: 4.3–48.1%) than in both 7-day therapy groups A (63%, 95% CI: 38.4–83.7%) and B (91%) ($P = 0.030$ and 0.001, respectively). As expected, the difference between the 7-day therapy groups A and B was not significant.

Despite the very short duration of the 1-day treatment, the sheer amount of medicines to be taken, i.e. 15 tablets or capsules and four solutions, was not well accepted by most patients, although compliance was excellent. Among the 11 patients treated with the 7-day triple therapy after failure of the 1-day treatment, nine (82%) preferred the 7-day therapy and two (18%) the 1-day therapy.

Our results were surprisingly disappointing when compared to those of other studies using a 1-day therapy comprising the same dose of amoxycillin, the same dose of bismuth subcitrate, a lower dose of proton pump inhibitor and the same dose of metronidazole instead of clarithromycin. As described in the literature, the two major factors contributing to the failure of H. pylori treatment are low compliance and the presence of a strain resistant to one of the antibiotics used. In our study, the compliance, measured by pill counts and interview a few days after the treatment, was high, reaching 100% in the 1-day therapy group (Table 2). Furthermore, in this therapy group, 10 patients (67%) received their medications in the hospital and five took them at home (of which two were cured).

In vitro resistance to antibiotics was not evaluated in our study. However, to date, resistance to amoxycillin and bismuth salts has very rarely been recorded and prevalence of clarithromycin-resistant strains has been found to be 3–10% in Europe (same in Switzerland). Consequently, it seems that H. pylori resistance to antibiotics could have played only a minor role in the 80% failure of the 1-day therapy. In addition, 91% of patients who failed with the 1-day quadruple therapy were eradicated after 7-days of triple therapy consisting of amoxycillin, clarithromycin and lansoprazole (triple therapy group B).

As elimination half-life of clarithromycin is no shorter than that of metronidazole, the failure of the 1-day therapy cannot be attributed to the use of the former antibiotic.

Smoking does not appear to be a factor in treatment failure either, the rate of smokers in the 1-day therapy group being the same (20%) in cured patients and in non-cured patients.

Finally, the substitution of metronidazole by clarithromycin was clearly not successful in terms of eradication rates.

In conclusion, we do not recommend the 1-day therapy used in this study for eradication treatment of H. pylori, essentially because of its poor efficacy, but also because of its poor acceptance by the patients and the numerous side-effects observed.
